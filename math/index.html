Formulas & Assumptions

1K-REF · show your working

Every number this site shows you comes from one of the formulas below. They are all standard acoustics and audio engineering — nothing here is invented, and nothing is fitted to data. They live as pure functions in a single file, assets/js/audio-math.js, which you are welcome to read, check, and copy.

The formulas matter less than the assumptions underneath them. Most audio maths is exact in a physics textbook and approximate in a room with a PA in it, so each section below says plainly what it takes for granted and where it starts to lie to you. Treat all of it as planning numbers, not measurements. A calculator tells you what to expect; a measurement microphone tells you what happened.

Shared Constants

used by more than one module

Speed of sound in air

c(T) = 331.3 + 0.606 · T c = speed of sound, m/s T = air temperature, °C c(20 °C) = 343.4 m/s ≈ 1127 ft/s

This is the linear approximation to the exact ideal-gas relation c = 331.3 · √(1 + T/273.15). Every module that needs a speed of sound uses this same function, so a wavelength read off 1K-NOTE and a room mode read off 1K-RT60 agree with each other.

What this assumes
  • Dry air at sea level. Humidity raises c by only a few tenths of a m/s at concert temperatures, so it is ignored.
  • Temperature, not pressure. For an ideal gas the speed of sound depends on temperature alone, so altitude barely moves this number — the thinner air at altitude is also colder, and that is what changes it.
  • The linear form drifts at extremes. Against the exact relation it is dead-on at 0 °C, off by 0.2 m/s at 20 °C, and off by 0.8 m/s (0.23%) at 40 °C. Between −20 °C and +40 °C the error stays under 1 m/s, which is far smaller than the error in guessing the room's temperature in the first place.
  • Modules that show a temperature field use yours. Ones that don't (wavelength readouts on the generator and note pages) assume 20 °C and say so on the panel.

Wavelength and period

λ = c / f T = 1 / f λ = wavelength, m f = frequency, Hz T = period, s

Both are exact by definition. The only assumption they carry is whichever c went into them.

Signal Generator

1K-GEN · open the module

Frequency dial

f = 20 · (20000 / 20) ^ (pos / 1000) pos = fader position, 0…1000

A logarithmic taper, so each octave takes the same amount of travel — the same reason graphic EQs are laid out that way. The typed field accepts 440, 1.5k, or 16khz and is clamped to 20 Hz – 20 kHz.

Level

gain = 10 ^ (dBFS / 20) applied to a source whose peak amplitude is 1.0
What this assumes
  • The fader is peak dBFS, not RMS. A sine at 0 dBFS peak measures −3.01 dBFS RMS. The fader defaults to −18 dBFS, which is quiet enough to plug in safely and hot enough to see on a meter.
  • dBFS here is digital full scale at the browser output. It is not a calibrated output voltage and it is not SPL. Your OS volume, your interface's output trim, and your amp all sit between this number and the air. Set gain structure with a meter, not with this fader.
  • Noise colours are matched to each other, not to the tones. All four are normalised to the same RMS (0.2, about −14 dBFS RMS) so switching between them changes the timbre and not the level. A tone at the same fader setting will be louder.
  • Sample rate is whatever your browser picked — usually 48 kHz, occasionally 44.1. Nothing here goes near Nyquist, so it doesn't matter.

How each signal is made

Sine, Tri, Square, Saw
Native Web Audio oscillators. The browser band-limits the harmonic-rich shapes, so they don't alias, but their top harmonics roll off approaching Nyquist — a 5 kHz square is not a mathematically perfect square.
White
Uniform random samples. Flat power per hertz.
Pink
Paul Kellet's filter approximation, −3 dB/octave (equal energy per octave). It tracks true pink within a fraction of a dB across the audio band — close enough for setting a system, not a laboratory reference.
Brown
Leaky integral of white, −6 dB/octave.
Blue
First difference of the pink sequence. Differentiating adds +6 dB/octave, turning −3 into +3 dB/octave.
Sweep ↑ / ↓
An exponential ramp across 20 Hz – 20 kHz in 8 seconds, chained so it repeats seamlessly. Exponential means constant time per octave: about 0.8 s for each of the ~10 octaves.
Warble
The tone frequency-modulated ±6% at 6 Hz. That is almost exactly ±one semitone, which is enough spread to stop the tone parking in a single standing-wave null while you walk a room.
Pulse
The tone gated on and off at 2 Hz, 50% duty. The gate is instantaneous, so every edge is broadband — that click is exactly what makes it good for finding which box is which, and exactly why it isn't a clean tone burst.
Two things the noise generator is not
  • The noise buffers loop (2 s for white and brown, 4 s for pink and blue). A looped buffer is strictly a periodic signal, so its spectrum is a dense comb at multiples of 0.25–0.5 Hz rather than truly continuous. Inaudible, but a long-window FFT will see it.
  • The oscilloscope is not a measurement. It taps the signal this page generates, after the output switch. It shows you what is being sent, never what your room, speaker, or microphone did with it.

Delay Time

1K-DLY · open the module
t = d / c samples = t · fₛ t = time, s d = distance, m c = speed of sound at your temperature fₛ = sample rate, Hz round trip = 2t

At 20 °C sound covers about 2.91 ms per metre, or 0.89 ms per foot — which is where the old "a foot per millisecond" rule comes from. It is accurate to about 11%, and the temperature correction is why the same delay ring sounds right at load-in and smeared at doors.

What this assumes
  • A straight line through open air. No diffraction around structures, no reflection paths, no sound travelling through the stage deck (which is faster and arrives first).
  • Still air at a uniform temperature. Outdoors neither holds. Wind adds or subtracts its own speed along the path, and a hot field under cooler air bends sound upward — both audible over a long throw to a delay tower.
  • Acoustic propagation only. This is the flight time through air. It does not include your console latency, network audio latency, amplifier DSP, or speaker processing. Add those separately; on a modern digital rig they are easily worth several milliseconds.
  • Alignment is not just time. Getting the delay right lines up arrival; it does not fix polarity, phase response through the crossover region, or level. Time alignment is one of the three.

Level Converter

1K-LVL · open the module
dBu = 20 · log₁₀(V / 0.774597) dBV = 20 · log₁₀(V / 1) dBFS = dBu − alignment V = RMS volts 0.774597 V = √(1 mW × 600 Ω), the definition of 0 dBu alignment = the analogue level your converter calls 0 dBFS, in dBu

dBu and dBV differ by a fixed 2.2185 dB forever, because they are just two different reference voltages. So consumer −10 dBV nominal is −7.78 dBu, which is 11.78 dB below the +4 dBu pro nominal — the real reason that DJ mixer never seems loud enough.

What this assumes
  • RMS volts of a sine wave. Feed it programme material and the peak-to-RMS relationship changes with the crest factor of whatever you are sending.
  • Voltage transfer, not power transfer. The 600 Ω in the definition of dBu is historical, from telephone lines. Modern gear bridges — a high-impedance input across a low-impedance output — so no 600 Ω load is implied and none should be connected.
  • dBFS is not absolute. There is no universal analogue level for 0 dBFS; it is a property of your converter. Common alignments are +24 dBu (SMPTE RP155, typical in the US), +18 dBu (EBU R68, typical in Europe), and +20 dBu (common in Japan). Pick the one your interface actually uses — guess wrong and every dBFS figure is off by 6 dB.
  • Headroom here is arithmetic, not a spec. "Alignment minus nominal" tells you the margin your converter allows above nominal. It says nothing about where your preamp, console bus, or amplifier actually clips, which are usually lower.

Room Acoustics

1K-RT60 · open the module

Reverberation time (Sabine)

RT60 = 0.161 · V / (S · ᾱ) V = room volume, m³ S = total boundary area, m² ᾱ = average absorption coefficient, 0…1 0.161 = 24 · ln(10) / c, the metric Sabine constant

Schroeder frequency

fₛ = 2000 · √(RT60 / V) below fₛ the room behaves modally; above it, statistically

Room modes (Rayleigh)

f(p,q,r) = (c / 2) · √( (p/L)² + (q/W)² + (r/H)² ) L, W, H = room dimensions, m p, q, r = mode indices, 0, 1, 2, … one index non-zero = axial two = tangential three = oblique

Axial modes bounce between one opposed pair of surfaces and carry the most energy, which is why they are the ones worth chasing with traps first. Tangential and oblique modes are progressively weaker. Modes bunched at nearly the same frequency are worse than the same number spread evenly.

What this assumes
  • Sabine over-predicts in absorbent rooms. It assumes a perfectly diffuse field and breaks down as absorption rises. Compared with the Eyring formula it reads about 3% long at ᾱ = 0.05, 8% long at 0.15, 12% at 0.2, 23% at 0.35, and 39% at 0.5. Above roughly ᾱ = 0.2, treat the answer as an optimistic upper bound.
  • Absorption is spread evenly. Sabine assumes it. Real rooms put all of it on one wall and the ceiling, which makes the decay direction-dependent in a way a single number cannot express.
  • One broadband ᾱ stands in for a frequency response. Real materials vary enormously with frequency — 25 mm of foam might be 0.9 at 2 kHz and 0.1 at 125 Hz. A single average will not predict the bass decay that is usually the actual problem.
  • Air absorption is ignored. Above about 2 kHz in a large room, air itself absorbs meaningfully and the real RT60 comes in shorter than this.
  • The room is an empty rectangular box with rigid walls. People, seating, curtains, and gear all absorb; an audience can halve the RT60 of a hall. Non-rectangular rooms don't have this mode series at all, and real walls flex, which damps and shifts modes downward.
  • The mode table is truncated. Indices up to 6, frequencies below 300 Hz, and the first 48 rows shown — enough for the range where modes are audible individually.

Note ↔ Frequency

1K-NOTE · open the module
f = A₄ · 2 ^ ((m − 69) / 12) m = 69 + 12 · log₂(f / A₄) cents = 1200 · log₂(f₂ / f₁) m = MIDI note number A₄ = reference pitch, default 440 Hz (ISO 16) MIDI 69 = A4 MIDI 60 = C4 one semitone = 100 cents ≈ 5.95%
What this assumes
  • Twelve-tone equal temperament, always. Every semitone is an identical ratio of 2^(1/12). Just intonation, meantone, and historical temperaments will disagree with this by up to ~20 cents on some intervals.
  • No stretch tuning. Real pianos are tuned progressively sharp at the top and flat at the bottom to match string inharmonicity, by as much as 30 cents at the extremes. This page shows the mathematical grid.
  • Octave numbering is the scientific convention, where MIDI 60 is C4. The MIDI specification never defined octave names, so some manufacturers — Yamaha most notably — call that same note C3. The frequency is identical either way; only the label moves.
  • Wavelengths on this page use 20 °C. Use 1K-DLY if you need a different temperature.

SPL & Power

1K-SPL · open the module

SPL at a distance

SPL = sensitivity + 10 · log₁₀(W) − 20 · log₁₀(d) sensitivity = dB SPL at 1 W / 1 m W = watts d = distance, m −6 dB per doubling of distance

Power for a target SPL

W = 10 ^ ( (target + headroom + 20 · log₁₀(d) − sensitivity) / 10 )

Because power is a 10·log quantity, every 3 dB of headroom doubles the amplifier you need. That is the arithmetic behind "we can't just turn it up" — 10 dB more SPL is ten times the power, and eventually the answer is a bigger PA rather than a hotter amp.

Speaker cable voltage drop

Rₗₒₒₚ = 2 · length · Rₓₑₛ ₘₑₜₑₛ loss (dB) = 20 · log₁₀( Z / (Z + Rₗₒₒₚ) ) Pₓₐ₋ₓₑ = I² · Rₗₒₒₚ, where I = √(W / Z) Z = nominal load impedance, Ω length = one-way run resistance table = solid copper at 20 °C, per conductor

The cable and the speaker form a voltage divider, so whatever fraction of the resistance lives in the cable is the fraction of your amplifier you paid for and did not use. Keep the loss under about 0.5 dB and stop worrying about it.

What this assumes
  • Free field, one source, on axis. Anechoic conditions with nothing to reflect off. Indoors the reverberant field adds energy, so real SPL at the back of a room is higher than this predicts, not lower.
  • Inverse square law, which line arrays do not obey. A point source loses 6 dB per doubling of distance. A line array behaves closer to a line source in its near field and loses nearer 3 dB per doubling, so this calculator will badly under-read for arrays at distance.
  • One box. Coherent sources sum; two identical boxes are up to 6 dB louder, not 3. Arrays, subs in cardioid, and coupled stacks all need their own maths.
  • No air absorption. Over a long throw the top octaves lose real level to the atmosphere (see ISO 9613-1), so a distant seat is quieter and duller than this suggests.
  • Sensitivity is the manufacturer's number, usually measured anechoically, on axis, and quoted optimistically. It is often stated as 2.83 V/1 m rather than 1 W/1 m — identical only into 8 Ω, and 3 dB apart into 4 Ω.
  • No power compression. Voice coils heat up and lose sensitivity under sustained power — commonly 2–6 dB. Treat the computed SPL as a ceiling you will not quite reach.
  • Cable maths is DC resistance only. Skin effect is negligible at audio frequencies in these gauges, and cable inductance and capacitance don't matter over speaker runs. Amplifier output impedance is taken as zero.
  • The load is treated as a constant resistor. A real driver's impedance swings widely with frequency — a nominal 8 Ω box may dip near 5 Ω and rise past 40 Ω at resonance — so the true loss varies across the band. The nominal figure is the honest middle of it.
  • Copper at 20 °C, solid conductor. Resistance rises about 0.39% per °C, so cable baking on hot asphalt reads higher. Stranded cable of the same AWG runs roughly 2% higher than solid because the strands spiral.

Where These Come From

standard references

None of this is original work. These are the textbook sources for the formulas above, listed so you can check them against something that isn't a website you found on a weird domain name.

  • Sabine, W. C. — Collected Papers on Acoustics (1922). The reverberation formula.
  • Eyring, C. F. — "Reverberation Time in 'Dead' Rooms", JASA (1930). The correction for absorbent rooms.
  • Schroeder, M. R. — "The Schroeder Frequency Revisited", JASA (1996). The modal / statistical crossover.
  • Rayleigh, J. W. S. — The Theory of Sound (1877). Rectangular-room modes.
  • ISO 16 — standard tuning pitch, A4 = 440 Hz.
  • ISO 9613-1 — absorption of sound by the atmosphere.
  • SMPTE RP155 and EBU R68 — converter alignment levels (+24 dBu and +18 dBu).
  • Standard copper wire tables — AWG resistance per 1000 ft for solid copper at 20 °C.
  • Paul Kellet's pink noise filter, from the musicdsp.org archive — the filter coefficients used by the noise generator.

Found a mistake? That's a genuine favour — the formulas are all in one short file and corrections are welcome on the issue tracker.
