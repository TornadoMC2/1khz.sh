Reading an RT60 Estimate

and where it lies to you

What RT60 actually measures

RT60 is the time a sound takes to decay by 60 dB after the source stops — from a shout to inaudible, roughly. Sabine's equation gets there from two numbers: the volume of the room and the total absorption in it. More air, longer tail; more soft surfaces, shorter tail. It is the oldest result in architectural acoustics and still the fastest way to know what kind of room you are standing in.

Where Sabine stops being trustworthy

The equation assumes a diffuse field: sound energy spread evenly, absorption spread evenly, a room that is not a corridor. Real rooms break all three. Once the average absorption coefficient goes much past 0.2, Sabine starts overestimating the decay time and Eyring's version is the better one. A single average α also hides the fact that absorption is strongly frequency-dependent — most treatment works well above 500 Hz and barely at all below 100 Hz, which is exactly why rooms that measure "fine" still sound boomy.

Room modes and the Schroeder frequency

Below the Schroeder frequency, a room does not have a reverberation time in any useful sense — it has individual resonances, standing waves between pairs of parallel surfaces, and the response is a comb of peaks and nulls rather than a smooth decay. Above it, the modes are dense enough to behave statistically and RT60 means something. The mode table tells you where those resonances sit. Axial modes are the loud ones and the ones worth treating first; tightly spaced clusters with big gaps between them are worse than an even spread.

Rough targets

For orientation rather than prescription: small control rooms and studios usually aim for 0.2–0.4 s, live rooms and rehearsal spaces 0.4–0.8 s, speech venues under about 1.0 s before intelligibility starts to suffer, and concert halls deliberately run 1.8–2.2 s. A room at 3 s is a room you will fight all night.
