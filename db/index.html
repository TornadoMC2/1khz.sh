dBu, dBV, dBFS: What Each One Means

reference levels

The two absolute ones

dBu and dBV both measure a real voltage, so a value in either one is a fact about a wire. 0 dBV is 1.0 V RMS, which is the sensible definition. 0 dBu is 0.775 V RMS, which is the voltage that dissipates one milliwatt into 600 Ω — a telephone-era convention that outlived the impedance it was based on. The gap between the two scales is a fixed 2.21 dB, always, in every direction.

The relative one

dBFS is different in kind: it measures how far below digital full scale a signal is, and full scale is not a voltage until somebody decides what it is. That decision is the alignment level, and it is not standardised across the industry. European broadcast (EBU R68) puts 0 dBFS at +18 dBu; American practice (SMPTE RP155, AES) usually puts it at +24 dBu. Same −20 dBFS reading, two different voltages on the output, six decibels apart. Set the alignment to match your converters before trusting any dBFS number here.

Headroom, and the −10 problem

Headroom is simply the distance from where you normally sit to where clipping starts. Nominal +4 dBu into a +24 dBu converter leaves 20 dB of room; the same signal into a +18 dBu converter leaves 14 dB. Consumer gear runs at −10 dBV nominal, which is −7.8 dBu, so plugging consumer outputs into pro inputs loses you about 11.8 dB before anything else has gone wrong.
