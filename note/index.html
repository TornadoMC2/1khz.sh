Pitch, Frequency, and MIDI

equal temperament

How the numbers relate

Twelve-tone equal temperament divides the octave into twelve equal ratios, so one semitone up multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of two — about 1.0595. An octave doubles it. A cent is a hundredth of a semitone, which works out to about 0.06% in frequency; most people notice a pitch error somewhere around 5–10 cents on a sustained note, and rather less than that on a unison.

The A4 reference

Everything is anchored to one number, and that number is a convention rather than a law of nature. ISO 16 fixes A4 at 440 Hz, which is what recording, film, and almost all popular music use. Plenty of European orchestras tune to 442 or 443 Hz for a brighter sound. Historically informed baroque performance usually sits around 415 Hz, close to a semitone flat. And 432 Hz has a devoted following on the internet built on claims about resonance and wellbeing for which there is no evidence — it is a fine thing to tune to if you like how it sounds, and nothing more than that. The reference field takes anything from 400 to 480 Hz.

MIDI note numbers

MIDI numbers the semitones from 0 to 127, with A4 at 69 and middle C at 60. The catch is that the octave name for middle C is not universal: the MIDI specification and most modern software call it C4, but Yamaha has historically called it C3. If a sampler is mapping everything an octave off, this is almost always why — the note number is unambiguous, the name is not.

Wavelength

The wavelength shown alongside each note is that frequency in air, and it is the number that makes acoustics intuitive. A low E on a bass at 41 Hz is over eight metres long, which is why no amount of small-room treatment controls it. A 10 kHz sibilance problem is 34 mm, which is why moving a microphone an inch fixes it.
